# Self-Introduction

## Who I am
I am a student working with history and computation. Outside the seminar room I am the kind of person who spends a Saturday turning a messy archive finding aid into a tidy data table, and then spends Sunday wondering whether the tidiness is actually an act of violence against the mess. I am interested in the friction between what institutions record and what they leave out.

## What I study, and what interests me
I study history, with a growing attention to the tools that produce historical knowledge as much as to the knowledge itself. What draws me in is the gap problem: the records that survive are never a neutral sample of the past, and the more I learn the more I notice how the shape of an archive — its silences, its categories, its filing order — quietly decides what can later be said. I am interested in the history of knowledge and the history of bureaucracy, essentially: how order gets made, enforced, and resisted.

## What I hope to do with AI in historical research
I want to use AI to work at a scale that close reading cannot, and to do so without losing the humility that close reading teaches. Concretely, I hope to use it to trace themes and patterns across bodies of text that no single researcher could read in a lifetime — letters, petitions, newspaper runs, court records — and, more importantly, to find the places where the pattern breaks, because that is usually where the interesting history is hiding. I hope AI can help me read the archive's edges rather than just its center.

## What worries me about AI
What worries me about AI is not that it will be wrong, because historians are used to wrong sources; it is that it will be wrong in a plausible, confident, well-sourced-looking way that outruns our ability to check it. A wrong citation is one thing, but a smoothly generated narrative that *feels* right is the kind of thing that gets reproduced uncritically and becomes hard to dislodge. In historical research specifically I worry about a compounding effect: models trained on digitized and already-curated collections will quietly bake in the same exclusions the archives themselves contain, laundering institutional bias into the appearance of objective synthesis. And beyond the archive, I worry about the same technology being used to automate judgment about real people — about who is hired, who is released, who is worth trusting — with the pattern-matching that worked fine on old newspapers and fails catastrophically on a person's life. The thing I genuinely lose sleep over is not machines becoming more human, but humans deciding they no longer need to check.

## One AI project I wish I could finish by the end of the term
I wish I could build a system that reads one archive — say, thousands of letters written by people who were never famous, never important enough to be the subject of any book — and lets me ask it questions not with keywords but with worries: *what are these people afraid of that they could not say out loud?* I want it to surface not the summary of what is written but the reconnaissance about what is *not* written, the hesitations, the deferrals, the moments where a letter shifts from report to plea. I want to watch it fail to find an answer and tell me precisely *why* it failed, so that the boundary of the archive becomes visible in the tool itself. I am fully aware this is probably not buildable and possibly not even coherent, but I do not care about that right now. I would rather end the term having built a beautiful, honest failure than another polished tool that makes the archive feel emptier than it is.
